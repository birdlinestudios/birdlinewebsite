What Is Return on Production Spend?

ROAS tells you what your media did. ROPS tells you what your creative did. Here is how we calculate it, and why it changes what you shoot next.

November 4, 2026·6 min read
Key takeaways
  • ROPS = tracked revenue from the creative ÷ what it cost to make.
  • It puts production on the same scoreboard as media.
  • A library of footage makes ROPS climb with every new cut.

Most brands can tell you their ROAS to the decimal. Ask what the creative behind those ads returned and the room goes quiet. The production invoice was paid months ago, the footage is on a drive somewhere, and nobody has connected the two numbers.

Return on Production Spend, or ROPS, is the number that connects them. It asks one question: for every dollar you spent making the creative, how much tracked revenue did that creative generate?

The formula

ROPS is tracked revenue attributed to a piece of creative, divided by the cost of producing it. If a shoot cost $20,000 and the ads cut from it drove $400,000 in tracked revenue, the ROPS is 20. Spend a dollar making it, get twenty back.

Two things make the number honest. First, the revenue has to be attributed at the ad level, which is why the Birdline App connects to the ad platform rather than asking anyone to type in totals. Second, the production cost has to include everything: pre-production, the shoot day, post, talent, the lot. A ROPS built on half the costs is a flattering guess.

Why ROAS is not enough

ROAS measures the efficiency of media spend. Put a dollar in Meta, get three out. It is a good number, and it is blind to the thing that moved it. Two campaigns with the same media budget can return wildly different ROAS, and the difference is almost always the creative.

Because ROAS ignores production, it quietly pushes teams toward cheap creative. The spreadsheet rewards the $500 edit and the $20,000 shoot equally, as long as the media math works. ROPS fixes the scoreboard. A shoot that produces six months of winning ads looks exactly as good as it is.

Production is not a cost line above the campaign. It is the campaign, measured.

What a good ROPS looks like

There is no universal benchmark, and anyone who gives you one is selling something. What we watch is the trend. A production that is cut once and shelved has a ROPS that stops moving the day the campaign ends. A production that feeds a library keeps earning: a new hook here, a seasonal cutdown there, a product shot reused in a static. The numerator keeps growing while the denominator stays put.

That is the pattern behind the partnerships that run for years. Public Rec has been cutting from a growing library since 2021. Gardyn’s hero shoot became a TV spot, a summer cut, a UGC-style series and a dozen statics. Their ROPS is not a snapshot; it is a curve, and it bends up.

How to start measuring it

  • Record what each production actually cost, all in, and tie it to a campaign.
  • Tag the ads cut from that production so revenue can be attributed back to it.
  • Review the number on a cadence, not once. Monthly is enough to steer by.
  • Let it change decisions: recut what is working, reshoot what is not, and stop paying for creative nobody can measure.

If your team does this by hand, it works; it is just slow.
